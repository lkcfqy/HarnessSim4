Paired Force-Safety Tests and a Direct MuJoCo Audit}
\author{Anonymous Authors}
\date{}

\begin{abstract}
Cable-connector insertion couples visual misalignment, cable load, frictional
contact, and damage-sensitive parts. We present ContactBelief, a contact-state
recovery controller, and a paired protocol separating algorithmic from
robot-execution evidence. The 2.5-D benchmark evaluates eight policies on 600
shared scenarios (\PrimaryEpisodes{} episodes). In the hard condition,
ContactBelief achieves \HardOursSuccess\% verified, undamaged success, versus
\HardDirectSuccess\% for direct insertion and \HardSpiralSuccess\% for spiral
search. The paired gains of \HardDeltaDirect{} and \HardDeltaSpiral{} points
remain significant after Holm correction. Removing orientation updates lowers
success to \HardNoOrientSuccess\%; removing retract lowers it to
\HardNoRetractSuccess\%. Guarded admittance reaches \HardGuardedSuccess\%
success with lower mean peak force, and ContactBelief does not significantly
outperform it. A \MuJoCoEpisodes{}-episode direct MuJoCo audit uses current
observations and native contact termination: ContactBelief reaches
\MuJoCoOursSuccess\% success and \MuJoCoOursDamage\% damage, versus
\MuJoCoDirectSuccess\% and \MuJoCoDirectDamage\% for direct insertion. These
results support contact-state recovery under two synthetic physics abstractions,
not real connector reliability, torque-level control, hardware safety, or
factory readiness.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textit{Note to Practitioners---}
Small terminal-pose errors, cable drag and friction can turn automated connector
insertion into a jam or a damaged part. This study evaluates a recovery sequence
that detects unexpected contact, unloads the terminal, updates alignment and
then verifies locking. In the synthetic benchmark it is substantially safer
than blind direct insertion and spiral search, but it does not significantly
outperform a carefully tuned guarded-admittance controller. The practical
message is therefore not to replace a reliable force-control baseline with an
unverified learned controller. A deployment should first measure connector
clearance, insertion force, latch behavior and cable reaction for each product
family, then compare controllers on paired fixtures while recording peak force,
damage, cycle time, retries and a post-insertion pull test. The MuJoCo robot in
this paper is a task-space digital twin, not evidence of production reliability.
The proposed hardware gate specifies the measurements required before factory
claims are made.

\section{Introduction}

Insertion is often presented as a rigid peg-in-hole problem, yet an electrical
connector at the end of a wire harness is not simply a peg.  Upstream cable
bending creates a load that changes with approach depth; the grasped terminal
can rotate; socket chamfers and latches create discontinuous contact; and a
successful-looking insertion must still be locked and verified.  Classical
compliance and force control provide the right low-level language
\cite{whitney1982quasistatic,hogan1985impedance}, but the high-level question
remains: when should a robot keep inserting, when should it infer edge contact,
and which corrective state should follow?

Learning-based insertion has demonstrated variable compliance, force-only
policies, visual servoing, residual control, and simulation-to-real transfer
\cite{beltran2020variable,markert2023force,haugaard2021visual,
johannink2019residual,tang2023industreal}.  Recent work also targets unseen
industrial connectors and deformable cable insertion
\cite{nair2022learning,wu2025dloassembly}.  Those systems motivate hardware
experiments that this paper does not yet possess.  Our immediate goal is more
modest and falsifiable: construct an auditable synthetic benchmark in which the
same latent physical scenario is presented to each controller, define damage
and lock verification before the formal run, retain strong classical and oracle
comparators, and test whether the controller behavior survives a second physics
abstraction with native contacts.

ContactBelief is a finite-state engineering controller, not a learned policy and
not a Bayesian posterior.  Its ``belief'' is the controller's internal discrete
estimate of free-space approach, guarded insertion, edge contact, retraction,
realignment, and verified seating.  Contact wrench signs update the visual
alignment target; a retract state unloads the guide before the next probe.  The
paper's contribution is the controller/evidence package rather than a claim that
this state machine supersedes modern learning systems.

The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a paired 2.5-D connector-insertion benchmark with clearance, friction,
  cable drag, visual and force bias, contact wrench, jamming, damage, retry, and
  six-step lock verification;
  \item eight frozen policies, three difficulties, 200 common-random-number
  scenarios per difficulty, exact paired tests, bootstrap intervals, and
  orientation/retract ablations;
  \item a collision-enabled terminal and socket in MuJoCo
  \cite{todorov2012mujoco}, with controllers observing native state and contact
  force directly over 300 additional paired episodes; and
  \item an episode-disjoint public-data ingestion audit plus a
  \AuditChecks-check independent evidence audit that prevents offline or
  synthetic metrics from being reported as hardware performance.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Compliance and force-guided insertion.}
Remote-center compliance and impedance control established that suitable
mechanical or commanded compliance can convert contact into alignment rather
than wedging \cite{whitney1982quasistatic,hogan1985impedance}.  High-level
search, force regulation, and insertion remain sensitive to contact stiffness.
Lee and Pham demonstrate one robust controller across a 10--100~N/mm stiffness
range, illustrating why a strong force baseline is necessary
\cite{lee2020stiffness}.  ContactBelief is evaluated against guarded admittance
and does not outperform it significantly in our hardest primary condition.

\paragraph{Learning assembly behavior.}
Variable-compliance reinforcement learning can combine a nominal trajectory,
force response, and learned residual action \cite{beltran2020variable}; residual
RL similarly retains an engineered controller while learning corrections
\cite{johannink2019residual}.  Force-only offline deep Q-learning has reported
tight-clearance insertion on hardware \cite{markert2023force}.  Continuous
visual servoing can reduce the uncertainty region before compliant contact and
avoid blind search \cite{haugaard2021visual}.  Our baselines isolate these
ingredients---vision without force, spiral search, guarded force recovery, and
two state-machine ablations---without claiming to reproduce their full systems.

\paragraph{Connectors, deformable cables, and simulation.}
Industrial insertion policies can be adapted to unseen connectors by online
practice \cite{nair2022learning}.  Wu et al. explicitly address a cable-held
Type-C connector and exploit contact during curriculum RL
\cite{wu2025dloassembly}.  IndustReal shows substantially stronger evidence than
ours: contact-rich simulation, policy transfer, and a real assembly system
\cite{tang2023industreal}.  Simulation nevertheless has a reality gap, especially
for stiff contact.  We therefore keep the 2.5-D and MuJoCo results separate,
never pool them, and reserve hardware claims for future experiments.

\section{Contact-State Method}

\subsection{State and sensing}

The primary state is $s_t=(x_t,y_t,\psi_t,\theta_t)$: axial insertion depth,
lateral displacement, yaw, and pitch.  A 30-mm terminal maps orientation to a
tip offset
\begin{equation}
  e_t=y_t+0.030\psi_t+0.45(0.030)\theta_t,
\end{equation}
and the collision envelope is
\begin{equation}
  E_t=|e_t|+0.38(0.030)(|\psi_t|+|\theta_t|).
  \label{eq:envelope}
\end{equation}
The controller observes biased/noisy pose $\hat{s}_t$, axial force $F_x$,
lateral force $F_y$, and contact torque $\tau_c$.  Bias is fixed within an
episode; noise is a frozen time sequence shared by all policies with the same
seed.  This pairing prevents one controller from receiving an easier draw.

\subsection{Contact update and recovery}

During free-space approach, visual servoing moves the observed pose toward an
internal target $(\bar y,\bar\psi,\bar\theta)$.  An edge-contact event is declared
when lateral force exceeds 0.9~N or axial force approaches the scenario's jam
threshold.  Contact load is
\begin{equation}
  L_t=\max\{|F_y|,F_x-5\},
\end{equation}
and the target shifts opposite the lateral contact sign,
\begin{equation}
  \bar y\leftarrow \bar y-\operatorname{sgn}(F_y)
  \operatorname{clip}(0.00055L_t,0.0010,0.0042).
\end{equation}
Torque sign also shifts yaw and pitch targets.  The controller retracts for
$8+\lfloor3d\rfloor$ steps at difficulty $d$, realigns in unloaded space, and
then resumes a slower probe.  The guarded-admittance comparator uses a higher
force threshold, lateral-only correction, and a shorter retract.  The
no-orientation ablation preserves contact and retract but omits angular target
updates.  The no-retract ablation corrects while loaded; its proxy increases
effective contact load by 1.45 and accumulates damage impulse above 8~N rather
than 10~N, representing sliding friction and edge pressure.

\subsection{Contact, jam, damage, and success}

For clearance $c$, violation is $v_t=\max(0,E_t-c)$.  Near the socket, contact
force is a stiffness-scaled function of $v_t$ and penetration, added to a
friction, drag, and insertion-speed seating load.  If total load exceeds the
scenario jam threshold, axial motion is attenuated until retraction unloads the
contact.  Damage occurs when either peak force exceeds a scenario limit or the
integral of high force exceeds a scenario impulse limit.  Success requires
40~mm depth, $E_t\leq0.9c$, no damage, and six consecutive lock-verification
steps.  An episode ends at verified success, damage, or 320 steps (6.4~s).
These equations are engineering proxies, not a finite-element connector model.

\section{Paired Evaluation Protocol}

\subsection{Scenario distribution}

Difficulty changes uncertainty rather than policy parameters.  Clearance is
sampled from $[7.5,10.5](1-0.43d)$~mm.  Friction is
$U(0.14,0.28)+dU(0.20,0.42)$; contact stiffness is 1.5--2.1~kN/m multiplied by
$1+0.35d$.  Initial lateral error spans
$\pm(13+15d)$~mm; yaw spans $\pm(0.10+0.20d)$~rad and pitch 80\% of that range.
Visual-bias and noise scales grow with $d$, as do three cable-drag components.
Jam, peak-force, and impulse limits vary per scenario.  The exact equations,
seed schedule, and pilot history were frozen in a protocol before the formal
run.

For each $d\in\{0.2,0.5,0.8\}$, 200 latent scenarios are constructed from base
seed 83,000,000.  Eight policies receive every scenario: ContactBelief, guarded
admittance, vision-staged without force, spiral search, direct insertion, the
two ablations, and a state-aware oracle.  The oracle reads latent pose and is an
upper reference, not a deployable comparator.  There are no exclusions.

\subsection{Endpoints and statistics}

The primary endpoint is verified, undamaged success.  Secondary endpoints are
first-pass success, damage, jam occurrence, peak force, cycle time, retries, and
failure type.  Per-policy success and mean force receive deterministic
10,000-draw nonparametric bootstrap intervals \cite{efron1979bootstrap}.
Within each difficulty, ContactBelief is compared with seven policies using
exact paired McNemar tests \cite{mcnemar1947sampling}; paired force uses a
one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test \cite{wilcoxon1945individual}.  Holm
correction is applied separately across all 21 success tests and all 21 force
tests \cite{holm1979simple}.  Effect sizes and intervals are reported even when
tests are not significant.

\subsection{Public-data auxiliary audit}

We retain the MIT-licensed LeRobot ALOHA simulated-insertion dataset as an
explicitly mismatched public proxy \cite{lerobotAlohaDataset}.  Its hashed cache
contains \PublicFrames{} finite state--action frames from \PublicEpisodes{}
complete 500-frame episodes.  A seeded whole-episode split uses
\PublicTrainEpisodes{} episodes for fitting and \PublicTestEpisodes{} for test,
with no shared episode.  A 30-input multi-output ridge residual model reaches
held-out action RMSE \PublicRidgeRMSE{}, versus \PublicIdentityRMSE{} for
$a_t=s_t$ (\PublicRMSEGain\% lower).  This validates data ingestion, splitting,
and an offline action interface only.  The rigid dual-arm simulation has no
automotive wire terminal, cable reaction, camera observation, or contact force;
it is excluded from every success and force claim below.

\subsection{Difficulty exposes the contact gap}

Easy scenarios are nearly saturated: ContactBelief and the oracle reach 100\%,
while direct and visual-only insertion reach 99.5\%.  At medium difficulty,
ContactBelief reaches 94.5\%, compared with 90.0\% guarded, 83.0\% spiral, and
80.5\% direct/vision-only.  Hard difficulty separates the methods: ContactBelief
reaches \HardOursSuccess\% (95\% CI 86.0--94.0), direct and vision-only each
\HardDirectSuccess\%, and spiral \HardSpiralSuccess\%.  ContactBelief's damage
rate is \HardOursDamage\%, compared with \HardDirectDamage\% for direct
insertion and 53.5\% for spiral search.

The oracle remains 100\% at all difficulties and therefore shows that the task
distribution itself is solvable.  ContactBelief is still ten points below that
ceiling in the hard condition, leaving a meaningful estimation/control gap.

At hard difficulty, ContactBelief succeeds on 69 seeds where direct insertion
fails; direct succeeds on only two reciprocal seeds.  This yields a
\HardDeltaDirect-point effect (bootstrap CI 26.5--40.0) and
$p=\HardDirectHolm$ after correction.  Mean peak force is
$|\HardDirectForceDelta|$~N lower (paired CI 1.39--1.65~N).  Against spiral
search the success gain is \HardDeltaSpiral{} points.  Thus the main conclusion
is not merely that recovery takes longer: it increases verified success while
reducing the high-force burden of force-blind insertion.

Removing orientation update reduces hard success from \HardOursSuccess\% to
\HardNoOrientSuccess\% (\HardDeltaNoOrient{} points, Holm $p=0.011$) and raises
mean peak force by 3.8~N (Holm $p=1.43\times10^{-6}$).  Removing retract reaches
\HardNoRetractSuccess\%; the six-point decrement and 1.3-N force increase do not
remain significant after correction ($p=0.186$ and 0.075).  We therefore claim
strong primary evidence for orientation correction but only directional primary
evidence for the retract state.

Guarded admittance is the important counterexample to overclaiming.  It reaches
\HardGuardedSuccess\%, only four points below ContactBelief; the paired CI
includes zero and Holm $p=1$.  Its mean peak force is 0.6~N lower, while mean
cycle time is 0.59~s longer.  The formal result supports parity-to-small-gain
against this strong baseline, not superiority.

\section{Direct MuJoCo Contact Audit}

\subsection{Independent closed loop}

The second backend uses a 50$\times$12$\times$12-mm collision-enabled terminal,
four guide rails, a backstop, and a compliant weld to a commanded gripper target.
Each scenario varies the 3-D start pose, opening, friction, visual/force bias,
and noise.  The controller observes the current MuJoCo body/site pose and native
\texttt{mj\_contactForce} values at every outer-loop step.  Contact-aware methods
have a physics-rate force stop; direct insertion does not.  The 2.5-D trajectory,
contact event, force, and outcome are never replayed.

Twenty paired seeds at each difficulty and five policies give
\MuJoCoEpisodes{} episodes.  A seated terminal must remain within axial,
lateral, vertical, yaw, and pitch tolerances for five updates without crossing
a scenario damage-force proxy.  The KUKA and Robotiq meshes are visual; the
audit remains task-space control.  The cable visual/dynamic chain is attached to
the kinematic gripper target and does not transmit its full reaction force into
the compliant terminal.  This is an explicit limitation.

\section{Limitations and Hardware Gate}

The result is a research draft, not a submission-ready hardware paper.  First,
the 2.5-D contact equations and damage thresholds are engineered proxies and
were calibrated through retained pilot runs.  The formal seeds are disjoint
from pilots, but the protocol was not registered externally.  Second,
ContactBelief is hand-designed; its state is not a probability distribution and
it has not been trained against diverse connector families.  Third, the direct
MuJoCo socket is deliberately generous and simplified.  It omits latch
mechanics, plastic deformation, pin-level geometry, measured camera latency,
force-sensor bandwidth, robot torque limits, and a cable-to-terminal reaction
path.  Fourth, a synthetic damage event is not evidence that a real connector
would be damaged at that force.  The public ALOHA audit is also a rigid simulated
task-mismatch control, not real insertion evidence.

A credible hardware gate should freeze at least three connector/cable families,
measure actual clearance and force limits, randomize fixture and grasp pose,
calibrate camera and six-axis force/torque sensing, and preregister success,
peak force, cycle time, jam, part damage, and a post-insertion pull test.  The
paired unit should be the same physical fixture perturbation across policies,
with at least 100 trials per key comparison and no unreported exclusions.
Guarded admittance must remain the primary classical comparator.  Only after
passing this gate should the paper make claims about factory reliability,
service life, labor substitution, or payback.

\section{Reproducibility and Audit}

The primary protocol freezes distributions, seeds, hypotheses, and multiplicity
handling before its 200-seed-per-difficulty run.  A separate MuJoCo protocol
records nine retained calibration iterations and freezes the collision fixture
before the 20-seed formal audit.  Episode CSVs, aggregate CSVs, paired tests,
protocols, sources, scene XML, representative trace, and image are SHA-256
hashed.  The public dataset manifest, nine source files, portable cache, episode
split, model, and offline metrics are audited separately.  An independent script
also recomputes row counts, unique paired keys, seed schedules, scenario
invariance, success/damage invariants, every aggregate, bootstrap interval,
exact McNemar statistic, Holm correction, and paired Wilcoxon result.  All
\AuditChecks{} checks pass.  Generated tables and figures are accepted only from
that passing audit.

\section{Conclusion}

Contact-triggered target updates, orientation correction, unloading, and retry
substantially improve a cable-connector insertion proxy over force-blind direct
and spiral approaches.  The strongest result is paired: at hard difficulty,
ContactBelief gains 33.5 points over direct insertion while lowering peak force.
Orientation update is supported by corrected ablation tests; retract is
directional in the primary benchmark and strongly safety-relevant in the direct
MuJoCo audit.  Guarded admittance matches or nearly matches ContactBelief in both
backends, so it remains the honest baseline to beat.  The next scientific step
is not another synthetic percentage.  It is a preregistered, force-calibrated
hardware study with real connector latching and cable reaction loads.
